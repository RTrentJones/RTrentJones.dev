I spent the first few years of my career at Fulgent Genetics, building software for clinical
genomics, and a lot of that work was about helping scientists look at the evidence behind a variant
call. When I built [BAMCP](/bamcp/), an MCP server that lets a model inspect sequencing reads, the
feature I cared about most was its pileup viewer: the aligned reads at a locus, rendered inline in
the conversation, the way a curator would look at them.

I assumed a model would judge variants better if it could see that picture. This post is about
testing that assumption over five rounds. It didn't hold, and the improvement that did hold came from
somewhere I wasn't looking.

## How the rounds worked

Each round showed a set of sites to three Claude models of different sizes and asked the same two
questions: is there a variant here, and is it real or an artifact. A site could be shown as the raw
reads in text form, as BAMCP's structured evidence, as the rendered pileup, or as a combination. The
score was joint accuracy, both answers right.

The evaluation plan for each confirmatory round was committed to the repository before any model
saw a site: the items, the conditions, the hypotheses and the tests. Every run was a fresh agent
that could read only its own input files, the answers were sealed until all runs finished, and the
transcripts were audited afterwards. Real sites came from the Genome in a Bottle HG002 sample, with
truth from its benchmark calls and the coordinates disguised so a model couldn't look them up. The
plans, data and results are all in BAMCP's
[`docs/eval`](https://github.com/RTrentJones/BAMCP/tree/main/docs/eval).

I'm summarising the main findings here; the
[results write-up](https://github.com/RTrentJones/BAMCP/blob/main/docs/eval/visual-results.md) has
every number, interval and caveat.

## Rounds 1 and 2: the picture was hard to read

The first round used 26 synthetic sites. Raw text was right 95% of the time and the picture alone
82%. The picture failed most on homozygous variants, getting them right in only one case in six,
and the reason turned out to be the rendering. Every base was coloured, so a column where every read
carried the same non-reference base was just another coloured column.

<PileupColours />

Round 2 redrew the viewer with grey reads and only mismatches coloured. On the same sites the
picture alone rose to 91%, and homozygous variants from one in six to five in six. Because that round
was designed after seeing which sites failed, I treated it as encouraging rather than settled.

## Round 3: the reads as text were the best evidence

Round 3 was the first real test: fresh synthetic sites plus 36 real HG002 sites, under a plan
registered in advance with three confirmatory comparisons. None of the three was supported. On the
real sites the reads as text were right 99% of the time, and adding the picture to them changed
nothing measurable.

The picture on its own was usable but weaker, at 87–89%, and it was overconfident when it was wrong.
Model size mattered most here. The largest model read pictures almost perfectly, and the smallest
got about seven in ten of the real sites right from the picture alone.

## Round 4: looking for sites where text struggles

If text is already near the ceiling, a picture has nowhere to help. So round 4 went looking for real
sites where a model reading text would get only about 70% right: the classes that trip up short-read
variant callers, such as low-fraction variants, caller false positives and variants in duplicated
genes. In pilots, those sites weren't hard at all. Models were right 94% of the time from raw
`samtools mpileup` output and 96% with a careful text summary.

Only one kind of site was hard: insertions and deletions inside long runs of a single repeated base,
where the sequencer's own slippage produces reads that look like a real variant. Round 4's main run
used 60 fresh sites of that kind, half real variants and half slippage. Neither BAMCP's text tools
nor the picture improved accuracy over raw `mpileup`, and both made the smallest model miss more
real variants.

There was one thing the picture did that text didn't. When they could see the reads, the larger
models called the genotype correctly, one copy or two, two to three times as often. That was a secondary
measure, and the first sign in four rounds that the picture added something.

## What BAMCP's text was getting wrong

Looking at why BAMCP's own text tools hadn't helped explained the genotype result as well. Two things
in BAMCP's curation summary were misleading the models on these sites.

It raised a high homopolymer warning on every indel inside a run of four or more identical bases,
whether or not the variant was real, so the warning carried no information. And it measured the
variant's share against every read at the site, including the many reads that start or stop partway
through the run and so cannot show how long it is. Those reads count as reference, which pulls a
heterozygous variant, present in about half the reads, down to something that looks like a
low-fraction one.

<SpanningReads />

The picture had been supplying the missing information: a model could see which reads crossed the
whole run. So I changed BAMCP to use only those reads. The homopolymer warning now appears only when
the called length is rare among reads that span the run, and the variant's share is measured among
them. Re-run on the same 60 sites, BAMCP's text took accuracy from 84% to 91%, all three models
improved, and correct genotypes on real variants went from 13 of 90 to 42 of 90.

Those were the sites that had prompted the change, so the result could easily have been a fit to
them. It needed a fresh test.

## Round 5: confirmation on sites no round had seen

Round 5 was registered before any site was drawn: 90 new sites, from four more chromosomes plus
fresh draws from round 4's, none within 10 kb of a round 4 site. It had two pre-registered
hypotheses, that BAMCP's text would improve accuracy and genotype calls over raw `mpileup`. Both
were supported. Accuracy rose from 83% to 89%, with each model improving by a similar amount, and
correct genotypes from 30 of 135 to 54 of 135, with no loss of specificity.

The gain came from heterozygous variants, which the models now called heterozygous far more often. Homozygous variants in long runs are still usually called
heterozygous, because the reads that stop inside the run dilute every fraction a model sees. That is
the next thing to fix.

## What it changed

The viewer is for the person. A model working through BAMCP gets the evidence as text, and the
viewer is there so the human in the conversation can see what the model is reasoning about. The
evaluation also changed the viewer itself, which is now grey with only mismatches coloured.

The more useful lesson for me was about the method. Most of what I learned came from the rounds that
didn't confirm what I expected. Writing the plan down first is what made those results usable: when
round 3 came back null, there was no reading of it that rescued the picture, and when round 5 came
back positive, there was no question of which analysis to believe.

There are limits worth stating. Every model in the evaluation was a Claude model, each site was run
once per model, the early rounds were synthetic, and the hard sites are one narrow class of variant.
The result is that, for indels in long repeats, BAMCP's text now helps models more than raw reads
do. It isn't a general claim about pictures, or about models reading genomic data.
